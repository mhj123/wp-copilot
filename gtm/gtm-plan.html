Shipping Work Copilot as a WordPress plugin.

Every AI chat app gives you a list of conversations. That is not organisation — it is a pile. This is the filing system.

8 August 2026 Repo: mhj123/work-copilot @ a4cf254 129 features catalogued 23 launch blockers

Four decisions that gate everything else

Each of these changes what the other workstreams do. Nothing downstream should start until they are settled — and all four are cheapest to make right now, at zero installs.

  1. The name. “Copilot” is Microsoft’s in this exact product category. Renaming touches every prefix, table, taxonomy and REST namespace — trivial today, a migration nightmare after a thousand installs.
  2. Plugin or plugin + theme. Today the workspace UI lives in the theme, so “install the plugin” does not currently give anyone a working product. This decides the shape of the whole codebase.
  3. What ships in v1. Roughly a third of the repo is two unrelated products. The scope cut is in the companion spreadsheet and needs your sign-off.
  4. Whether hosted installs are in the launch story. Recommendation: not as a product, but capture the demand from day one.
01

Name and brand

The current name is a trademark risk in precisely the category it competes in, and a rename is close to free today.

Why “Work Copilot” cannot ship

Microsoft has spent three years and a great deal of money making “Copilot” mean “an AI assistant inside a software product” — GitHub Copilot, Microsoft 365 Copilot, Copilot Studio. An AI assistant plugin called Work Copilot is the confusion case that trademark law exists to prevent.

WordPress.org enforces this independently of any rights holder. Its guidelines prohibit plugin names that infringe trademarks, and plugins have been made to rename post-approval for far weaker collisions. That risk is survivable at zero installs and severe at ten thousand.

The rename reaches further than the display name: the wcp_ prefix, the wcp_context taxonomy, the wcp_heading post type, five wp_wcp_* tables, the work-copilot/v1 REST namespace and every option key. That is exactly why it belongs in the same pass as the scope cut, before a single external install exists.

What the name has to carry

  • It becomes a platform, not just a plugin — add-ons will be named after it, so it must extend gracefully (Name Voice, Name Graph).
  • It should point at structure and grounding, not at chat. The competition owns “chat”.
  • Short enough for an admin menu item, distinct enough to search for.
  • Clean on: WordPress.org slug, the .com, a GitHub org, and a trademark register search in your jurisdiction. Run all four before committing — the shortlist below is a starting point, not a clearance.

Shortlist

GroundworkRecommended
Two meanings, both exactly right. Grounding is the technical term for what the product actually does — generation grounded in your own corpus rather than the model’s memory. Groundwork is the foundational preparation that makes later work fast. It keeps a thread back to “Work”, extends cleanly to add-ons, and says “structure” without saying “AI”. Watch for: it is a common English word, so slug and domain availability are the real test.
ThroughlineRunner-up
The thread that connects everything — which is literally what missions and canonical references do across your corpus. More distinctive and more ownable than Groundwork. Watch for: abstract; it needs the tagline to do more work.
Almanac
A structured, indexed reference that accumulates over time. Fits the learning loop and the atomic-item model well. Watch for: connotes annual and calendrical, which is not what this is.
Atrium
A structured open space you move through, with everything arranged around it. Calm, architectural, distinct from the AI-tool naming crowd. Watch for: says nothing about work or knowledge on its own.

The brand system around it

  • Tagline: Structure for AI work. Five syllables, no jargon, and it plants a flag against the pile-of-chats status quo.
  • Positioning line: “An open-source workspace where your AI assistant files its work into your structure — on your own server, with your own API key.”
  • Add-on convention: Name + noun, always. Voice, Graph, Bookmarks, Calendar. Never a cute sub-brand — the point of add-ons is that they obviously belong to the same system.
  • Keep the vocabulary you already use. Pages, items, contexts, missions, the corpus. It is consistent, it is already in the code, and “corpus” in particular is a genuinely good word that no competitor uses.
  • Visual identity: you need a 128px and 256px icon and a 772×250 and 1544×500 banner for the plugin directory before you can submit. Budget this as real design work — the banner is the first thing every prospective user sees.

The scope cut

The companion spreadsheet catalogues all 129 features with a proposed action for each. Here is what it adds up to.

The workbook (gtm/work-copilot-feature-inventory.xlsx) has a row per feature, each citing the file it lives in, what it does, its maintenance burden, its known launch risk, and a proposed action. There is a yellow column for your decision and a summary tab that counts them live.

Six possible actions, and the distinction that matters most is between flagging off and cutting. Nothing recommended for a feature flag is bad — it is unfinished, redundant with something better, or too advanced for someone’s first ten minutes. Flagging preserves the work at zero onboarding cost.

The single biggest clarity win

Wiretap (crypto and equity influencer tracking, trade plans, earliness scoring) and OpenBiografy (biography generation from documents) are roughly 5,500 lines of two entirely different products, for two entirely different audiences, sitting in the repository a prospective user will browse. Wiretap additionally carries a financial-recommendation surface that no one wants attached to this launch.

Cutting them to a private repo costs the launch nothing and removes the single most confusing thing about the project. Two ideas inside them are worth porting back into core later: Wiretap’s AI spend tracking, and OpenBiografy’s extract-then-reconcile pattern.

Feature flags do not exist in the codebase yet. Building that layer is a prerequisite for this workstream, not a consequence of it — see workstream 04.

04

Launch-critical features to build

Not in the code today, and the launch does not work without them. Ordered by dependency.

Prerequisites

  • A feature-flag layer. Every “flag off” decision in the spreadsheet depends on this existing. A filterable registry plus a settings screen for advanced users. Build it first.
  • uninstall.php and schema versioning. Clean removal of five tables and all options; an admin_init version check that re-runs dbDelta when the stored version is behind.
  • A capability gate. The product is documented as single-user but not enforced as single-user. On a multi-author site today, that is a data-exposure question you do not want asked for you. Gate everything behind one explicit capability and fail closed.

The first-run experience

This is where the launch is won or lost. A fresh install is an empty WordPress site, and an empty knowledge tool teaches nobody anything. Four pieces:

  • A setup wizard. Paste your Anthropic key → a real Test connection call that reports what actually came back → choose a starter structure → done. Optional OpenAI key, clearly labelled as unlocking corpus-wide context.
  • A starter corpus. One click installs a small worked example: an Areas/Projects tree, a page carrying a written protocol, and half a dozen items. This exists so that Action plan from context — your single most differentiated feature — produces something impressive within two minutes of install, instead of failing quietly against an empty index.
  • Empty states that teach. Every surface with no content should say what goes there and offer the one action that fills it. Cheap to build, disproportionate effect on activation.
  • Lean on the “Onboard” action you already have. It greets, summarises and suggests. That is the product explaining itself in its own voice — make it the wizard’s final step.

Trust and cost, which are the same thing here

  • An API spend meter. Bring-your-own-key means the user pays per call, and unexplained bills generate support load and uninstalls in equal measure. Wiretap already has a “Runs & Budget” screen — port the idea into core: tokens and estimated cost per action, per day, with an optional cap.
  • A “what was sent” disclosure. One click on any AI response to see the exact context pack that went to the model. It is a debugging tool, a privacy answer, and the most persuasive possible demonstration that context scoping is real.
  • A verified export round-trip. “No lock-in” is a claim until export → fresh install → import reproduces the corpus exactly. Test it, then say so in the marketing copy.
  • Page mission on the front end. Move it out of wp-admin. It is a headline differentiator currently hidden in the place the pitch says you never have to go.

On hosted installs

Do not build hosting for v1. It converts a product problem into an operations business overnight — uptime, backups, per-tenant key custody, support — and you would be running it while the plugin is still finding its shape.

Instead, put a waitlist form on the site from day one. It costs an afternoon, and the signup rate against download volume tells you whether hosting is a real business or a thing people say they want. If the answer is yes, a managed WordPress host partnership is a faster route than building it yourself.

Business model, for the record: open-source core, free on WordPress.org — paid add-ons are the revenue line, hosted installs are the option you keep open. That model only works if the free core is genuinely complete, which is another argument for the scope cut in workstream 02.

06

Documentation and onboarding

The product is conceptually unusual. Pages, headings, contexts, items and missions is five new ideas before anything happens, and documentation is how that cost gets paid down.

Build the documentation site in the product

The single best move available here: run the public docs site on the plugin itself. It is simultaneously the documentation, the live demo, the SEO surface, and the proof that the thing is real — a visitor reading “pages hold items, items carry contexts” is looking at a page holding items carrying contexts. It also means you dogfood every rough edge before your users find it.

Six documents, in this order

  • Install and connect. Five minutes, screenshotted, including what a working “Test connection” looks like and what each key unlocks.
  • Core concepts. One page, one diagram. Pages are contexts, headings are sub-contexts, items are atoms that can live in several places at once. If someone only reads one page, this is it.
  • Your first workspace. A worked example building an Areas/Projects tree from nothing, ending with a chat that demonstrably knows where it is.
  • Writing missions and protocols. The highest-leverage document you will write. It is the analogue of an AGENTS.md guide, and it is what turns the product from a note-taker into something that works the way you work.
  • Getting the assistant to co-author. Action plans, accepting as nested items, saving chat messages as items, extracting memories — the learning loop, explained end to end.
  • Extending it. The add-on model, the REST surface, and the hooks. This is the document that recruits contributors.

A three-minute video, non-negotiable

This product is spatial — structure, nesting, a panel that files things where you are standing. Prose is the wrong medium for that, and no screenshot carries it. One tight demo: empty install → structure appears → chat that knows the page → action plan grounded in a protocol you wrote → the plan lands as real nested items. That video is the single asset every channel in workstream 07 will reuse.

Open-source hygiene

  • LICENSE, CONTRIBUTING.md, SECURITY.md with a disclosure address, a code of conduct, and issue and PR templates.
  • A real CHANGELOG.md kept in step with the readme.txt changelog.
  • GitHub Discussions rather than Issues for questions, so the issue tracker stays a work queue.
  • Rewrite the existing internal docs for an external reader. DOCUMENTATION.md is excellent raw material and currently addressed to you. PRD-COMPOUNDING-LOOPS.md stays private.
  • Keep AGENTS.md and the agent-os standards public. They signal to contributors — and to AI coding tools — that this repo has conventions.
07

Messaging and launch

The positioning, who to say it to, and what the posts actually say.

The one idea

Do not compete on assistant quality — you are calling the same models everyone else is. Compete on what happens to the output. Every AI product produces walls of text and then abandons them in a scrolling list. This one files them.

The line: “Your AI chats are a pile of unsorted text. This gives them a filing system — and lets the assistant do the filing.”

Three supporting proofs, in descending order of persuasiveness: an assistant that writes back into your corpus so the next conversation is better than the last; context scoped to where you are standing, working against a mission you wrote; and it runs on your server with your key, so nothing about it is rented.

Who this is actually for

  • Self-hosters. The strongest fit by some distance. Already run WordPress, already own their data, already have API keys, already suspicious of SaaS. They need the least convincing of anyone.
  • Knowledge-work operators — consultants, solo founders, agency owners. Live in projects and protocols, feel the pain of scattered chats acutely, but need the WordPress part to be invisible.
  • The PKM crowd. Obsidian and Logseq users who want an AI that participates in the vault rather than sitting beside it. Opinionated, vocal, and they will tell you exactly what is wrong — which is useful.

Sequencing

Phase 1
Decide, then cut
Settle the four gating decisions. Rename everything in one pass. Cut Wiretap, OpenBiografy, delegation and the personal scripts to a private repo. Build the feature-flag layer and flag off what the spreadsheet says. Nothing public happens yet.
Phase 2
Make it safe to publish
Every Critical blocker, then every High. Bundle the CDN libraries, audit all REST permissions, add uninstall.php and migrations, wire up the capability gate. This is the phase where the repo becomes publishable.
Phase 3
Make it survivable for a stranger
Setup wizard, starter corpus, empty states, spend meter, page mission on the front end. Then find five people who are not you, watch them install it cold, and fix whatever they trip over. Nothing else in this plan matters more than that observation.
Phase 4
Submit and write
Submit to WordPress.org early — review runs for weeks and you want that clock running while you work. Build the docs site on the product itself, record the demo video, make the directory assets, open the repo, put up the hosting waitlist.
Phase 5
Launch, in that order
r/selfhosted and r/WordPress first — friendliest audiences, and a live rehearsal for the questions. Fix what they surface. Then Show HN and Product Hunt on the same day, once approval has landed and the install is genuinely smooth. Save your one HN shot for the version that works.

What to measure

  • Activation, not installs. The number that matters is what fraction of installs configure a key and create more than ten items in week one. Installs are vanity; that ratio is the product working.
  • Return rate at day 7 and day 30. This is a habit product. If people do not come back, no amount of messaging fixes it.
  • Add-on attach rate. Directly tests whether the extensibility model is a business or a slogan.
  • Hosting waitlist signups per hundred downloads. The cheapest possible read on whether hosted installs are worth building.
  • Support volume by cause. Every repeated question is a documentation or onboarding bug. Track the causes, not the tickets.

Risks worth naming now

  • “WordPress” is a liability with the AI-tools crowd and an asset with self-hosters. Do not fight it — segment. Lead with WordPress on r/selfhosted and r/WordPress; lead with the workflow everywhere else and let WordPress be the reassuring detail further down.
  • Two API keys for the flagship feature is real friction. Corpus-wide context needs OpenAI as well as Anthropic. Either abstract the embeddings provider so one key can do both, or be very clear that it is optional — but do not let someone discover it halfway through setup.
  • Single-user is a hard ceiling on the consultant and agency segment. Fine for v1, but it will be the most common feature request from your most commercially interesting audience. Have an answer ready.
  • The frontier labs can ship a version of this. Projects and memory features already move in this direction. Your defensible ground is ownership and extensibility, not features — so keep saying “your server, your data, your add-ons”, because that is the part they structurally cannot copy.
  • One HN shot. A Show HN that lands on a rough install burns the channel for a year. The discipline is to hold it until phase 5, however tempting it is at the end of phase 3.
